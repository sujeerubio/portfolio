Project 1 of 7

Supersonic Flow & Sonic Boom Analysis

  • MATLAB
  • Compressible flow
  • Analytical modeling
Course
ME 399 Independent Study
Advisor
Prof. Neelesh Patankar
Team
Solo
Date
Mar – Jun 2026

Overview

Why did the Concorde's sonic boom get supersonic flight banned over land, and how much does an aircraft's shape control it? Over a 10-week independent study, I built a MATLAB model of the Concorde at Mach 2 and 52,000 ft from first principles. The model tracks the flow through every shock and expansion from nose to tail, predicts the N-wave pressure signature that reaches the ground, and shows how nose geometry changes boom strength. I then derived simplified reduced-order models and benchmarked them against the exact solution to find out which approximations actually hold up.

What I did:

  • Researched sonic boom physics, FAA overland restrictions, and NASA's X-59 low-boom shaping strategy.
  • Simplified the Concorde into a 2D nose–fuselage–tail geometry built from published dimensions.
  • Built MATLAB solvers for oblique shocks (θ–β–M relation, weak-shock root-finding) and Prandtl-Meyer expansion fans (iterative inversion of ν(M)).
  • Constructed the ground-level N-wave signature in both time and distance.
  • Ran parametric sweeps of nose deflection angle against shock strength.
  • Derived linear, logarithmic, and hybrid small-angle models from first principles and benchmarked each against the exact solution.

Goals & Questions

  • What pressure, temperature, and Mach number does the flow see at each stage along the Concorde's body?
  • What does the resulting sonic boom signature look like, and how strong and how long is it?
  • How sensitive is boom strength to nose angle, and where does the attached-shock model break down?
  • Can a cheap analytical approximation replace the exact shock and expansion equations? If so, which one?

Approach

1. Exact shock–expansion model. Starting from standard-atmosphere freestream conditions (M = 2.0, P = 10.6 kPa, T = 216.7 K), the flow passes through a nose oblique shock, two Prandtl-Meyer expansion fans, and a tail oblique shock. Each stage feeds the next.

State Event Mach Pressure (kPa) Temp (K)
1 Freestream 2.00 10.61 216.7
2 Nose oblique shock 1.60 19.15 258.1
3 Expansion fan 1 1.99 10.63 218.1
4 Expansion fan 2 2.43 7.69 181.0
5 Tail oblique shock 1.99 14.80 220.2

2. N-wave signature. I used the state pressures to construct the classic N-wave: a +8.5 kPa bow shock, a linear pressure ramp, and a recompression at the tail. The signature lasts about 105 ms (T = L/V). I also projected the shocks to the ground to plot the signature against distance, which spans about 5.5 km.

3. Nose-angle sweep. I swept the nose deflection angle from 0° to 90° and solved the full flow chain at each angle. The bow-shock pressure jump grows faster than linearly with nose angle. The sweep also locates the shock detachment limit at 22.9° for Mach 2. Beyond that angle, no attached-shock solution exists, and a curved bow shock stands off ahead of the nose. The tail pressure jump shows the opposite curvature because the expansion fans weaken the flow that reaches the tail shock.

4. Reduced-order models. I derived small-angle scaling laws, Δp/p ≈ ±A(M)θ and ΔM/M ≈ ∓B(M)θ, then tested three versions:

  • Linear: captured Mach number well, but the pressure error grew with every step and reached 66% by the tail.
  • Logarithmic: fixed most of the pressure error but over-amplified the Mach number change.
  • Hybrid (log pressure + linear Mach): cut the tail pressure error to 16% and predicted Mach within about 1%.

The reason is mathematical. A linear pressure step multiplies by (1 + a)(1 − a) = 1 − a², so even a perfectly symmetric compression–expansion pair loses pressure. The exponential form, e^a · e^−a = 1, cancels exactly.

Outcomes

  • Predicted a Concorde-class N-wave with an 8.5 kPa bow-shock jump and about 105 ms duration at the aircraft.
  • Identified the Mach 2 shock detachment limit at 22.9°. The Concorde's 11.1° nose sits at roughly half that limit.
  • Developed a hybrid reduced-order model that reduced end-state pressure error from 66% to 16% compared with a linear model, and explained why it works.
  • Connected the model to real low-boom design: highly swept wings (normal Mach ≈ 1.14 on the Concorde's 55° leading edge) and slender noses, the strategy behind NASA's X-59.

Limitations & What I'd Do Next

This is a 2D, inviscid, first-order model. It ignores lift, which adds to the real bow shock (Seebass & George), and it ignores atmospheric propagation and attenuation, so the pressures shown are near-field values, not ground overpressures (real Concorde ground booms are about 93 Pa). Next steps would be adding a lift term using the Carlson simplified sonic-boom method, adding r^(−3/4) far-field attenuation, and validating against CFD.

High-Power Rocket Structural Analysis

  • ANSYS Mechanical
  • MATLAB
  • OpenRocket
Course
ME 362 Stress Analysis
Team
3 students
My role
Flight-data analysis, structural FEA
Date
Mar – Jun 2026

Overview

A NUSTARS member's high-power rocket, built for a NAR Level 1 certification launch, needed to survive the worst aerodynamic load of its flight. Our team set out to prove it would before launch day. Instead of guessing at loads, we built a four-stage simulation chain. We measured the physical rocket and modeled it in CAD, simulated its flight in OpenRocket, solved the airflow at peak load in ANSYS Fluent, and imported that pressure field into ANSYS structural FEA.

My role: Teammates handled the CAD and CFD. I owned the two ends of the pipeline: turning the flight simulation into a load case, and turning the CFD pressures into a structural verdict.

  • Wrote MATLAB scripts to process OpenRocket flight data, compute dynamic pressure through the flight, and identify max-Q (26.4 kPa at Mach 0.62, t = 1.2 s) as the critical load case.
  • Ran ANSYS Static Structural FEA with the CFD surface pressures imported directly as loads, then evaluated deformation, von Mises, principal, and cylindrical (hoop, radial, axial) stresses.
  • Applied the Brittle Coulomb-Mohr failure criterion for the fiberglass composite and built a custom factor-of-safety contour across the whole rocket.

Requirements

  • No structural failure: stresses must stay below the strength of the epoxy/E-glass composite (UTS 440 MPa; UCS 352 MPa, estimated at 80% of UTS).
  • Factor of safety ≥ 1.5 to cover material variability, build quality, and simulation simplifications.
  • Limited deflection, especially at the fins, so the rocket stays stable in flight.

Approach

1. CAD from the real rocket. We measured every structural part with calipers and a scale and modeled it in Onshape: a von Kármán nosecone, body tube, coupler, motor mount, centering rings, and three fins. The rocket is 0.89 m long with a 57 mm body tube.

2. Finding the critical load case (my part). OpenRocket simulated the flight on an H182 motor and confirmed a stable configuration (1.06-caliber stability margin). I wrote MATLAB scripts to process the exported flight data, calculate q = ½ρv², and plot the altitude, velocity/Mach, and dynamic-pressure histories. Dynamic pressure spikes to 26.4 kPa just 1.2 s after launch, while the motor is still burning, then falls to nearly zero long before apogee at 1,042 m. That identified max-Q as the single worst-case instant and gave the CFD its exact boundary conditions.

3. CFD at max-Q. We ran a 3D steady, compressible Fluent model with k-ω SST turbulence, Sutherland viscosity, and pressure far-field boundaries. Peak pressure reaches about 111 kPa at the nose tip, and the solution converged in about 650 iterations.

4. Structural FEA (my part). I linked the Fluent results into ANSYS Static Structural in Workbench, so each surface sees its own location-specific pressure instead of an assumed uniform load. A fixed support at the base of the body tube gives the solver a reaction point. At zero angle of attack the loading is nearly axisymmetric, so this support adds little artificial bending, and I excluded the stress concentrations right at the constraint from the results. Because the composite is brittle, I used Brittle Coulomb-Mohr instead of von Mises as the failure criterion: n = (σ₁/σ_UTS − σ₃/σ_UCS)⁻¹, plotted as a contour over the full model.

Outcomes

  • Maximum von Mises stress of 3.7 MPa, about 1% of the material's tensile strength.
  • Maximum deformation of 18 µm, at the nosecone. That is far too small to affect the fins or flight stability.
  • Minimum factor of safety of about 83 (Brittle Coulomb-Mohr), far above the 1.5 requirement.
  • Conclusion: the rocket's structure is not the limiting risk at max-Q, which gave the builder confidence going into the certification launch.

Limitations & What I'd Do Next

  • The model treats the filament-wound fiberglass as isotropic and linear-elastic. A real layup is orthotropic, so the next step is orthotropic material properties from test data.
  • Max-Q is the worst aerodynamic load, but it isn't the only load. Motor thrust compression, ignition heating near the base, and parachute-deployment shock are likely more severe and should be analyzed next.
  • Validate the OpenRocket loads against real flight data from the launch.

Project 3 of 7

Gearbox Output Shaft Design

  • Machine design
  • Fatigue analysis
  • SolidWorks
Course
ME 315 Theory of Machines
Team
3 students
My role
Owned Shaft C, the output shaft
Date
Spring 2026

Overview

Our team designed a 70 kW two-stage spur-gear speed reducer that steps a 2,600 rpm motor down to 299 rpm. Each team member owned one shaft system. I designed Shaft C, the output shaft. It carries the gearbox's highest torque, 2,233 N·m, through a 55-tooth gear and delivers it to the driven machine through a coupling. My scope covered the full shaft system: loads, shaft sizing, keyways, gear check, bearing selection, and the SolidWorks part and assembly drawings.

What I did:

  • Derived gear mesh forces and bearing reactions in two planes from the drivetrain's power and speed.
  • Built shear and bending-moment diagrams to locate the critical cross sections.
  • Sized the shaft for infinite life using modified-Goodman fatigue and von Mises yield, with Marin endurance factors and keyway stress concentrations.
  • Designed DIN 6885 parallel keyways for the gear and coupling seats.
  • Selected SKF 6313 deep-groove ball bearings by L10 life analysis.
  • Checked Gear 4 for tooth bending and pitting using the AGMA method.
  • Modeled and drew the shaft, gear, and assembly in SolidWorks, specifying H7/k6 bearing and gear fits.

Requirements

  • Transmit 70 kW at 299.4 rpm with daily start/stop operation.
  • Achieve infinite fatigue life with a minimum safety factor of 1.3 at 99% reliability.
  • Bearings must last the 5-year duty life (14,560 hr) without replacement if possible.
  • Fit the shared gearbox housing: a 155 mm bearing face-to-face span and a 185 mm center distance to Shaft B.
  • Use AISI 1045 cold-drawn steel (Sut = 550 MPa, Sy = 310 MPa).

Design & Analysis

Loads. With T = 2,233 N·m on a 275 mm pitch-diameter gear, the mesh produces a 16.2 kN tangential and a 5.9 kN radial force. The gear sits off-center: 129 mm from the coupling-side bearing and 59 mm from the far bearing. As a result, the far bearing carries more than twice the load (11.9 kN vs. 5.4 kN). The peak combined bending moment at the gear is 699 N·m.

Two critical sections, two failure modes. I checked both the gear seat and the coupling seat. Each one governs a different failure criterion:

Section Diameter Loading Goodman FoS (fatigue) Yield FoS
Gear keyway Ø70 mm Bending + torsion 2.29 (governs) 3.07
Coupling keyway Ø60 mm Torsion only 3.77 2.13 (governs)

The gear seat sees the maximum bending moment, so it controls fatigue. The coupling seat carries no bending, but its smaller diameter raises torsional stress (τ ∝ 1/d³), so it controls yield. I used conservative keyway stress-concentration factors (Kf = 2.0, Kfs = 1.6) to cover the shoulder fillets as well.

Bearings. Because the gear is spur-cut, the bearings see pure radial load (X = 1, Y = 0). The required dynamic rating was 75.8 kN, and the SKF 6313 is rated at C = 97.5 kN. I used the same bearing at both positions so the housing bores match on each side wall. The governing bearing lasts about 31,000 hr (10.6 years), more than twice the duty life, so no bearing changes are needed.

Geometry & fits. The Ø70 gear seat steps down through locating shoulders to Ø65 bearing seats and a Ø60 coupling end. Bearings and gear use H7/k6 transition fits, and the keyways are end-milled with rounded ends.

Outcomes

  • Shaft C has infinite fatigue life, with a minimum FoS of 2.29 (fatigue) and 2.13 (yield) against a 1.3 requirement.
  • The bearing configuration needs zero replacements over the 5-year life, while the system level still requires one Shaft B bearing change.
  • I integrated my shaft into a complete 8.68:1 gearbox with shared housing constraints and a full maintenance schedule.

What I'd Improve

Design review caught an assembly issue in my first drawing. The gear seat sat between two fixed Ø75 shoulders, so the gear could not slide onto the shaft. The fix is to replace one shoulder with a spacer or retaining ring. This taught me to design for assembly order, not just stress.

3D-Printed Bicycle Brake Calipers

  • Topology optimization
  • FEA
  • SLS printing
Course
ME 240 Mechanical Design & Manufacturing
Team
5 students
My role
CAD & Manufacturing Lead
Date
Spring 2025

Overview

Our team designed, 3D-printed, and road-tested a pair of rim-brake calipers for a bicycle. We started from a mounting interface and a block of allowable design space. FEA and topology optimization guided the material removal, and the parts were printed in Nylon-12 by selective laser sintering (SLS). We then went through two rounds of design–build–test on a real bike. The final calipers weigh 35.5 g, less than a quarter of our 150 g target, and stop the bike within our 15 m limit.

My role (CAD & Manufacturing Lead):

  • Modeled the calipers in CAD, turning the topology-optimization results into printable left and right caliper geometry.
  • Updated the CAD for the second iteration: slip-fit pivot and pad holes, a new cable-mount nub, and enlarged cutouts and fillets.
  • Built the needs and metrics tables, turning 22 user needs into 20 measurable metrics with values traced to ISO 4210, federal bicycle regulations, and published data.
  • Helped run the topology optimization and FEA that guided where material came out.

Requirements

We built a needs table from user, safety, and performance considerations, then turned it into 20 measurable metrics backed by ISO 4210, CPSC/eCFR, and published data. The key ones:

Metric Ideal Marginal
Caliper mass 150 g 250 g
Stopping distance (dry) 8 m 15 m
Rider input force 50 N 70 N
Manufacturing cost $10 $30
ISO 4210 compliance (sharp edges, lock washers, adjustment) Yes Yes

Design Process

1. Design space → FEA. We modeled the full allowable volume around the brake mounts and loaded it. Almost all of it sat far below the 50 MPa allowable stress, which meant there was a lot of material to remove.

2. Topology optimization. The optimizer showed where load actually flows from the cable to the pivot to the pad. That gave us the organic, trussed shapes we used as a starting point.

3. First design. We redrew the calipers to follow the topology results, then smoothed sharp transitions to cut stress concentrations. FEA predicted about 4 mm of tip deflection against an 11 mm limit (a deflection safety factor of about 2.75). Physical testing later matched closely, with 3 mm on the upper arm and 5 mm on the lower.

4. Print, assemble, test. We SLS-printed the calipers in Nylon-12, mounted them on a bike, and measured mass, stopping distance, and the rider force needed to brake.

Testing & Iteration

Metric Iteration 1 Iteration 2 Target (marginal)
Mass 41.3 g 35.5 g (−14%) ≤ 250 g
Stopping distance 11.3 m (wheels locked) 14.8 m ≤ 15 m
Rider input force 68.9 N 102 N ✗ ≤ 70 N
Material cost $8.26 $7.10 ≤ $30

Iteration 1 problems:

  • The pivot and pad holes printed undersized and had to be drilled out.
  • The calipers twisted under load because the cable pulled off-axis from our mounting point. We hadn't designed for that load case.

Iteration 2 fixes:

  • Resized the holes for a proper slip fit, so assembly became quick and easy.
  • Added a cable-mount nub so the cable pulls straight, which eliminated the twisting completely.
  • Enlarged cutouts and fillets to save another 14% of mass.

Outcomes & Lessons

  • Both iterations passed the most important metric, stopping distance. Iteration 1 locked the wheels.
  • The cable-mount redesign solved the twisting problem and simplified assembly.
  • The key lesson: the extra weight reduction in iteration 2 was a mistake. Iteration 1 was already 70% under the mass target. Removing more material added compliance, which pushed the rider force to 102 N (failing the 70 N limit) and lengthened the stopping distance. Our recommendation was to go back to the stiffer iteration-1 body and keep the new cable mount and tolerances. I learned to optimize for the metric that matters, not for the one that's easiest to improve.
  • Production plan: casting for an entry-level bike (50k units/yr), forged and CNC-machined aluminum for a high-end road bike (10k/yr), and metal additive manufacturing for low-volume custom frames (200/yr).

Project 5 of 7

Benchtop Smoke-Wire Wind Tunnel

  • Prototyping
  • Flow visualization
  • SolidWorks
Program
Kappa Theta Pi capstone
Team
Solo, with a KTP mentor
Note
My first personal engineering project

Overview

Wind tunnels let engineers see how air moves around an object, but real ones are room-sized. For my KTP capstone, I set out to build a working one on a lab bench from scratch, with no kit and no instructions. I designed the airflow path, built a flow straightener out of straws, and used a smoke wire to make the airflow visible. Then I ran a bluff test body through it and watched the flow separate.

Why I built it: to turn my interest in aeronautics into something hands-on, build my CAD and mechanical design skills, and see the fluid dynamics I'd only read about.

How It Works

1. Air source. Two 120 mm-class computer fans, powered at 12 V from a bench supply, push air into the tunnel.

2. Flow straightener. Fans produce swirling, uneven air. I packed straws into a handmade honeycomb in front of the fans. Each straw acts as a tiny duct that forces the air to travel straight, the same idea as the honeycomb sections in full-size tunnels.

3. Test section. The test section is an open channel lined with black foam board, so the white smoke stands out on camera.

4. Smoke-wire flow visualization. I stretched a nichrome wire across the flow just downstream of the straightener and coated it in a glycerol-based fluid. At 12 V the wire heats up and vaporizes the coating into thin, evenly spaced smoke streaks. These streaklines show the path the air takes around the model.

5. Test model. I designed a TIE fighter model in SolidWorks, with a spline-profiled cockpit, angled hexagonal wing panels, and a full drawing set. It wasn't printed in time for testing, so I ran the tunnel with a sanded wooden block as a bluff-body stand-in. The TIE fighter was later printed in PLA on a Bambu printer.

What I Saw

  • The straightener worked. Smoke left the wire as distinct, roughly parallel streaks instead of a swirling cloud, so the straws were removing much of the fans' rotation.
  • Flow separation over a bluff body. The streaks stayed fairly organized approaching the block, then lifted over its leading edge and broke into a wide, churning turbulent wake behind it. This is the classic behavior of a blunt shape, and the reason streamlined bodies have much less drag.
  • Flow quality limits. Even upstream, the streaks wavered and spread out, which points to leftover turbulence from the fans and an open, low-speed test section. That told me exactly what to improve next.

What I'd Do Next

  • Add a contraction cone between the straightener and the test section. It would speed up the air and smooth it out, which is how real tunnels get clean, uniform flow.
  • Enclose the test section with a clear acrylic window, so room air doesn't disturb the flow.
  • Measure, not just observe: a pitot tube or anemometer for airspeed, and a small load cell for drag.
  • Test the printed TIE fighter against a streamlined shape to compare wakes.

Takeaway

This was my first engineering project outside of class, and it's where I learned to build first and refine from what I saw. Making flow separation visible with straws, foam board, and a hot wire gave me an intuition for aerodynamics that I've built on since, through compressible-flow research and rocket analysis.

The Under-Stand: Tandem Bike Kickstand

  • Human-centered design
  • Statics
  • Prototyping
Course
Design Thinking & Communication
Client
Envision Unlimited
Team
4 students
My role
Documentation, drawings, purchasing
Date
Winter 2024

Overview

Envision Unlimited runs tandem-bike rides for adults with intellectual and developmental disabilities. Their stock kickstands kept failing. Under the weight of a tandem and two riders they bent into an "M" shape or slid forward, some lasting only 1–2 rides at about $70 per replacement. The worst-placed one sat 0.5 in from the rear rider's heel while pedaling. Our team designed the Under-Stand, a detachable, telescoping kickstand that mounts high under the front seat instead of low near the drivetrain.

My role: The whole team designed together. I led the documentation and technical drawings, including most of the final report and the build and use instructions, and managed purchasing as the team's cardholder.

Users & Requirements

  • Strength: support a loaded tandem without bending or sliding.
  • Low fine-motor demand: riders and volunteers must deploy it with simple motions. No small screws or tricky levers.
  • Out of the way: nothing within either rider's pedaling range.
  • Portable: stows on the bike in under 30 in, total weight under 10 lb.
  • Budget: under $150, and cheaper than repeatedly replacing kickstands.

Design

The key idea: move the support point up. A stand anchored high on the frame resists tipping with much less force than a short stand near the ground. With the rod and both wheels touching the ground, the bike rests on a stable three-point base.

Statics check. We modeled the stand's ground reaction against tire friction. For the bike not to slide, μ·sin θ ≥ cos θ. We used the worst case, wet-tire friction (μ ≈ 0.4), and limited the stand to carrying no more than half the bike's weight. That gave a usable resting angle between 70° and 150°, which set the mounting angle.

Components:

  1. Telescoping aluminum tubes (1½ in and 1⅜ in OD, 0.038 in wall). They extend from 2 ft stowed to about 4 ft deployed, the same idea as a suitcase handle or mic stand.
  2. B-split collar lock with a quarter-turn knob. We chose it over a lever clamp because it takes the least strength and dexterity.
  3. PVC 45° wye connector that slides onto the seat post under the pilot's seat. Off-the-shelf pipe clamps only came in parallel or perpendicular, and the wye gave us the angle we needed.
  4. Moldable 3M Scotchfil putty lining so the connector grips the seat post without rotating, with no permanent modification to the bike.
  5. Rubber foot (μ ≈ 1.15) for grip on asphalt and gravel, plus a red warning band marking the tube's full extension.

Process

  • Research and interviews: We interviewed program staff and riders, and observed the bikes at their storage facility. Nudging one bike tipped it straight over.
  • Mockups: We built a tripod stand and an under-seat stand, and laser-cut an acrylic model of the bike to test different mounting heights. The tripod needed two people (one to lift the bike), so we dropped it.
  • Design review: Reviewers flagged connector rotation, so we added the putty lining and the rubber foot.
  • Prototype: We Dremeled and bandsawed the PVC wye to fit the tube, bonded the foot with JB Weld, and wrote step-by-step build and use instructions so Envision could make more.
  • User testing: Program participants tested the prototype on a real tandem and confirmed it met the strength and ease-of-use requirements.

Outcomes

  • A working kickstand that keeps the tandem upright and stays clear of both riders.
  • Under the $150 budget, and buildable by anyone with basic shop tools.
  • Next steps: a purpose-made connector instead of a ground-down PVC fitting, smoother telescoping action, a lock against the tube collapsing unexpectedly, and long-term wear testing on gravel.

The HandHug: Protective Hand Wrap

  • Human-centered design
  • Assistive technology
  • Sewing
Course
Design Thinking & Communication
Client
School occupational therapist
Team
4 students
My role
Design & Finances Lead
Date
Spring 2024

Overview

Our user was a medically fragile toddler with polymicrogyria, a rare brain condition. He is nonverbal, breathes through a tracheostomy, and has no voluntary finger movement. His hand was in his mouth 85–90% of the day, and constant saliva and biting had left the back of his hand cracked and callused. Mitts, splints, and teethers had all failed: they either restricted motion or still let his hand get wet.

The catch: hand-to-mouth contact is one of his main ways of soothing himself and exploring the world. So we had to protect his hand without taking that away.

My role (Design & Finances Lead): I sewed every hand wrap (wraps, Velcro, and snaps) and managed the $150 budget. I led the design discussions, while the team made material and design decisions together.

Requirements

Protection (client) Satisfaction (user)
Keep the injured area dry and prevent further damage Let the soothing habit continue
Full finger and wrist range of motion Don't restrict or upset him
Mouth-safe, no choking hazards, nowhere near the trach Comfortable (he dislikes cold)
Easy for caregivers to put on, take off, and wash

Testing Drove the Design

We brought three types of mockup to user testing: a cloth hand wrap, a fitted mitt, and two protective bibs.

  • The bib blocked contact entirely, which was overkill for a localized problem, and it would have to go over his existing bib.
  • The mitt was hard to put on, slipped off, and he stopped bringing his hand to his mouth at all. That meant it was taking away the soothing behavior.
  • The wrap stayed put and left his fingers free, and something unexpected happened. He ignored his exposed fingers and chewed the wrap's knot instead. At one point he used his other hand to push the knot into his mouth.

That accidental finding became the design: give him something better to chew than his hand.

Design

The HandHug is a soft wrap that covers the knuckles, with a detachable chewy toy where the knot used to be.

  • Terry cloth wrap: soft, absorbent, and machine-washable. We chose it over waterproof fabrics, which feel uncomfortable on skin, and over chamois.
  • Food-grade silicone chewy: durable 70A silicone, non-toxic, and textured for oral stimulation. We cut it to 2.75 in to match his knuckle span and halved its thickness so it bends with the hand.
  • Velcro closure: replaced the tied knot, which had frustrated him during testing, so caregivers can put it on and take it off in seconds.
  • Sew-on snaps: let the chewy and wrap separate for washing, which the client needed given his medical risk. They're strong enough to survive arm flailing but easy for an adult to undo.

Outcomes

  • The client said it could fit seamlessly into her daily routine. We couldn't test the final version on the user because he was hospitalized for seizures during the final weeks.
  • Under the $150 budget.
  • Next steps: a quantitative moisture test (a dropper test on the fabric), interchangeable chewies without removing the wrap, a more adjustable fit as he grows, and longer user-testing sessions.

What I Learned

When your user can't talk, observation is everything. The best feature of this design came from noticing an unexpected behavior during testing, not from our original concept.
